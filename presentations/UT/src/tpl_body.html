Slide 5 · Method

How do we calculate a data center’s value?

Peak electrical capacity (MW) is the one number that is always public. The figure below is live — hover any point.

__BRAND__

The key point

This measure of value includes both real property and personal property.

Real property — land, buildings, and improvements permanently affixed to the land.

Personal property — the servers, racks, cooling, and electrical equipment inside.

For an ordinary commercial business, real property dwarfs personal property. For a data center it is the reverse: personal property can be three to four times real property value. A valuation that counts only the building misses most of the asset — and Utah taxes both at 100% of fair market value.

Real vs. personal property magnitudes: Kem C. Gardner Policy Institute, Data Centers and Utah’s Property Tax, Aug. 2026, Fig. 2.

For peak capacity above 95 MW the relationship is well approximated by FMV = 0.0100181 × MW − 0.3496 ($ billions). Because that line predicts negative values below 34.9 MW, a three-parameter exponential is fit to capacities under 95 MW. Source: Evans, “Introducing DataCenterAtlas,” Econosseur; code in code/all/fig3tab1_MWtoFMV.py.

Introducing DataCenterAtlas.org

An explainer of the DataCenterAtlas.org web tool, its sources, and its calculations.

  • Data centers should be assessed based on their benefits relative to their costs, including externalities imposed on other businesses and residents. And data centers should be evaluated by the same criteria as all other businesses and investments across industries.
  • The DataCenterAtlas.org web tool provides a transparent, county-level tool that allows policymakers, local officials, and residents to compare the economic benefits, tax revenues, electricity demand, water use, and employment effects of data centers using publicly available data.
  • Property tax revenue from data centers can be sizable to counties and municipalities, even when significant tax exemptions are granted.
  • Data center water and electricity use are significant, but declining. And current data center water and electricity use are comparable to or lower than many other common uses across industries.
August 11, 2026

Which Localities Can Capture the Benefits of the Data Center Infrastructure Boom?

Data center issues at the county and municipal level — counties and municipalities should be competing for data centers.

  • Counties and municipalities should be competing for construction of data centers in appropriate locations within their boundaries.
  • Electricity is the biggest cost and constraint on local resources, and that bottleneck will exist independent of data center construction.
  • DataCenterAtlas.org highlights the significant benefits of data centers, especially in terms of property tax revenue and jobs.
  • The most common reasons for local opposition can be overcome with better information, more transparent decision processes, and a focus on aesthetics.

Utah Tax Policies Related to Data Centers

A great explainer of the tradeoffs with data center tax incentives. Recommend it to colleagues.

  • Lays out the structural vs. firm-specific choice cleanly: broad-based rules that treat all firms alike, versus targeted inducements for named entities.
  • Its advantages/disadvantages table is honest in both directions — inducements can make an infeasible project feasible, but may also misallocate resources, shift burden onto other taxpayers, and simply relocate activity that would have happened anyway.
  • Summarizes the actual toolkit: tax increment financing through CRAs and development authorities, sales tax exemptions on large data center equipment, and income tax credits.
  • Confirms the uniform-and-equal baseline: 100% of market value for business property, 0.92% statewide average rate.
August 10, 2026

Data Centers and Utah’s Property Tax

A good discussion of what a data center adds to the tax base — but read Figure 1 with care.

  • Strong on mechanics: real property enters as “new growth” and raises revenue; personal property does not, so it shifts burden between taxpayers and then “boomerangs” as equipment depreciates.
  • Headline finding: a $2 billion data center would exceed 20% of the property tax base in 21 of Utah’s 29 counties.
  • The caveat: that base blends commercial and residential property, and residential enters at 55% of market value — Utah’s 45% residential exemption. In residential-heavy counties the denominator is well below true market value, which inflates the data center’s apparent share of the base.
  • It is the right number for a Truth-in-Taxation revenue question; it is not the data center’s share of county wealth.
